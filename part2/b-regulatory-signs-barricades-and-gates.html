Part 2 · Signs · Chapter 2B

Regulatory Signs, Barricades, and Gates

The single most heavily used chapter in Part 2 for practicing engineers: STOP/YIELD signs and intersection right-of-way control, speed limit signs and California's statutory/E&TS speed-zoning framework, movement/lane control signs, passing and keep-right/keep-left signs, DO NOT ENTER/WRONG WAY/ONE WAY signing, parking/standing/stopping signs, pedestrian and traffic-signal regulatory signs, weight limit and truck signs, barricades and gates, and the California-specific R/SR-series signs.

2B.01Application of Regulatory Signs

Standard · Support

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 01Regulatory signs shall be used to inform road users of selected traffic laws or regulations and to indicate the applicability of the legal requirements.
  2. 02Regulatory signs shall be installed at or near where the regulations apply. The signs shall clearly indicate the requirements imposed by the regulations and shall be designed and installed to provide adequate visibility and legibility in order to obtain compliance.
  3. 03Regulatory signs shall be retroreflective or illuminated (see Section 2A.21).
  4. 04Traffic control device related orders, ordinances, and resolutions by local authorities which affect State highways shall be approved by Caltrans.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 05Signs required for enforcement are normally placed by, and at the expense of, the authority establishing the regulation.
  2. 06Refer to CVC § 21461 for failure to obey a regulatory sign.

2B.02Design of Regulatory Signs

Standard · Support

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 01Regulatory signs shall be rectangular unless specifically designated otherwise in this Manual. Regulatory signs shall be designed in accordance with the sizes, shapes, colors, and legends contained in the "Standard Highway Signs" publication and Caltrans' California Sign Specifications (see Section 1A.05).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 02The use of educational plaques to supplement symbol signs is described in Section 2A.09.
  2. 03The use of LEDs in the border or legend of regulatory signs is described in Section 2A.12.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 04LED signs displaying a part-time prohibitory message incorporating a red circle and diagonal of a static sign shall display a red symbol that approximates the same red circle and diagonal as closely as possible. The symbol of the action to be prohibited shall be displayed in white LEDs on a black background.
  2. 05A regulatory sign displayed entirely with LEDs and incorporated within the border of a larger full-matrix changeable message sign shall display the regulatory sign legend in the size, shape, color, and legend of the standard regulatory sign.

2B.04STOP Sign (R1-1) and ALL-WAY Plaque (R1-3P)

Standard · Support · Guidance · Option

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 01When it is determined that a full stop is always required on an approach to an intersection, a STOP (R1-1) sign (see Figure 2B-1) shall be used.
  2. 01aWhen a required stop is to apply at the entrance to an intersection from a one-way street with a roadway 30 feet or more in width, STOP signs shall be erected both on the left and right sides of the one-way street at or near the entrance to the intersection. Refer to CVC § 21355.
  3. 02Secondary legends shall not be used on STOP sign faces.
  4. 03The STOP sign shall not be displayed using a changeable message sign.
  5. 04At intersections where all approaches are controlled by STOP signs (see Section 2B.12), an ALL-WAY (R1-3P) supplemental plaque (see Figure 2B-1) shall be mounted below each STOP sign. The ALL-WAY plaque shall have a white legend and border on a red background.
  6. 05Supplemental plaques with legends such as 2-WAY, 3-WAY, 4-WAY, or other numbers of ways shall not be used with STOP signs.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 06The use of the CROSS TRAFFIC DOES NOT STOP (W4-4P Series) and other plaques with variations of this legend is described in Section 2C.66.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 07The TRAFFIC FROM LEFT (RIGHT) DOES NOT STOP (W4-4aP) plaque or ONCOMING TRAFFIC DOES NOT STOP (W4-4bP) plaque should be used at intersections where STOP signs control all but one approach, unless the only non-stopped approach is from a one-way street.
Option — Permissive
  1. 08The EXCEPT RIGHT TURN (R1-10P) plaque (see Figure 2B-1) may be mounted below the STOP sign if an engineering study determines a special combination of geometry and traffic volumes allows right-turning traffic on the approach to enter the intersection without stopping.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 09The design and application of Stop Beacons are described in Section 4S.05.
  2. 10A STOP (R1-1) sign is not a "cure-all" and is not a substitute for other traffic control devices and/or traffic calming strategies. Often, the need for a STOP sign can be eliminated if sight distance is increased by removing obstructions.

Through Highways

Option — Permissive
  1. 11STOP (R1-1) signs may be installed either at or near the entrance to a State highway, except at signalized intersections, or at any location so as to control traffic within an intersection. Refer to CVC §§ 21352 and 21355.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 12Authority to place STOP (R1-1) signs facing State highway traffic is delegated to the Caltrans District Directors.
Option — Permissive
  1. 13Local authorities may designate any highway under their jurisdiction as a through highway and install STOP signs in like manner. Refer to CVC § 21354.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 14No local authority shall erect or maintain any STOP sign or other traffic control device requiring a stop on any State highway, except by permission of Caltrans. Refer to CVC § 21353.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 15Caltrans will grant such permission only when an investigation indicates that the STOP sign will benefit traffic.

2B.05YIELD Sign (R1-2)

Support · Standard

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01The YIELD sign requires road users to yield the right-of-way to other traffic on certain approaches to an intersection or on a two-way approach to a one-way section of roadway, such as a narrow bridge or underpass. Vehicles controlled by a YIELD sign need to slow to a speed reasonable for existing conditions, or stop when necessary to avoid interfering with conflicting traffic.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 02The YIELD (R1-2) sign (see Figure 2B-1) shall not be displayed using a changeable message sign.

Signing for Right-of-Way at Intersections

2B.06General Considerations

Support · Guidance · Standard · Option

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Unsignalized intersections represent the most common form of intersection right-of-way control. Selection of control type might be impacted by specific requirements of State law or local ordinances.
  2. 02Roundabouts and traffic circles are circular intersection designs and are not traffic control devices. The decision to convert an intersection from a conventional intersection to a circular intersection is an engineering design decision, not a traffic control device decision. As such, criteria for conversion to a circular intersection are not included in the MUTCD.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03The type of traffic control used at an unsignalized intersection should be the least restrictive that provides appropriate levels of safety and efficiency for all road users.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 04Types of right-of-way control at an unsignalized intersection, from least to most restrictive: (A) No intersection control (Section 2B.09) — no right-of-way devices on any approach; (B) Yield control (Section 2B.10) — YIELD signs on all approaches (circular intersection), on opposing approaches (four-leg), on a single approach (three-leg), or in the median of a divided highway, placed on the minor road; (C) Minor road stop control (Section 2B.11) — STOP signs typically on opposing approaches (four-leg) or a single approach (three-leg), normally on the minor road (Section 2B.07 covers selecting the minor road); (D) All-way stop control (Section 2B.12) — STOP signs on all approaches.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 05When selecting a form of intersection control, the following factors should be considered: (A) motor vehicle, bicycle, and pedestrian traffic volumes on all approaches (where units/day or units/hour is indicated, it is the total of vehicle, bicycle, and pedestrian volume); (B) driver yielding behavior with regard to all modes of conflicting traffic, including bicyclists and pedestrians; (C) number and angle of approaches; (E) approach speeds; (E) sight distance available on each approach; (F) reported crash experience; and (G) the presence of a grade crossing near the intersection.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 06YIELD or STOP signs shall not be used for speed control.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 07Appropriate traffic calming or other speed control measures are available to control vehicle speeds without diminishing the effectiveness of traffic control devices used for their specified purpose.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 08Because the potential for conflicting commands could create driver confusion, YIELD or STOP signs shall not be used in conjunction with any traffic control signal operation, except: (A) if the signal indication for an approach is a flashing red at all times; (B) if a minor street or driveway is located within or adjacent to the signal-controlled area but does not require separate signal control because of extremely low conflict potential; or (C) if a channelized turn lane is separated from adjacent travel lanes by an island and is not itself signal-controlled.
  2. 08aSTOP signs shall not be erected at any entrance to an intersection controlled by traffic signals. Refer to CVC § 21355(a).
Option — Permissive
  1. 08bSTOP signs may be used at a channelized turn lane if it is separated from adjacent same-direction travel lanes by an island and is not itself signal-controlled.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 09STOP signs and YIELD signs shall not be installed on different approaches to the same unsignalized intersection if those approaches conflict with or oppose each other, except as provided in Items A and B of Paragraph 3, Section 2B.10.
  2. 10Portable or part-time STOP or YIELD signs shall not be used except for emergency and temporary traffic control zone purposes.
  3. 11A portable or part-time (folding) STOP sign manually placed into and removed from view shall not be used during a power outage to control a signalized approach unless the maintaining agency establishes that the signal indication to be first displayed to that approach upon power restoration is flashing red, and that the portable STOP sign will be manually removed from view before resuming stop-and-go signal operation.
Option — Permissive
  1. 12A portable or part-time (folding) STOP sign that is electrically or mechanically operated such that it only displays the stop message during a power outage, and ceases upon power restoration, may be used during a power outage to control a signalized approach.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 13The use of STOP signs at grade crossings is described in Sections 8B.04 and 8B.05.
  2. 14Section 9B.01 contains provisions regarding the assignment of priority where a shared-use path crosses a roadway.

2B.07Determining the Minor Road for Unsignalized Intersections

Guidance · Support

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01Selection of the minor road to be controlled by YIELD or STOP signs should be based on one or more of: (A) a roadway intersecting a designated through or numbered highway; (B) a roadway with the lower functional classification; (C) a roadway with the lower traffic volume; (D) a roadway with the lower speed limit; and/or (E) a roadway that intersects with a roadway of higher priority for one or more modes of travel.
  2. 02When two intersecting roadways have relatively equal volumes, speeds, and/or other characteristics, the following should be considered in selecting the minor road: (A) controlling the direction that conflicts the most with established pedestrian crossing activity or school walking routes; (B) controlling the direction that has obscured vision, dips, or bumps that already require lower operating speeds; and (C) controlling the direction with the best sight distance from a controlled position to observe conflicting traffic.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 03Refer to CVC § 21356 for the erection of YIELD signs upon the approaches to more than one of the intersecting streets.

2B.08Right-of-Way Intersection Control Considerations

Guidance

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01Before converting to a more restrictive form of right-of-way control at an unsignalized intersection, the following alternative treatments to address safety, operational, or other concerns should be among those considered: (A) where yield- or stop-controlled, installing Yield Ahead or Stop Ahead signs on the appropriate approaches; (B) removing parking on one or more approaches; (C) removing sight distance obstructions; (D) installing signs along the major street to warn approaching road users; (E) relocating stop line(s) and making other changes to improve sight distance; (F) installing measures to reduce approach speeds; (G) installing an Intersection Control Beacon (Section 4S.02) or Stop Beacon (Section 4S.05) to supplement STOP sign control; (H) installing a Warning Beacon (Section 4S.03) on advance warning signs on major-street and/or minor-street approaches; (I) adding one or more lanes on a minor-street approach to reduce per-lane volume; (J) revising geometrics to channelize vehicular movements and reduce movement completion time, which could also assist pedestrians; (K) revising geometrics to add pedestrian median refuge islands and/or curb extensions; (L) installing roadway lighting if a disproportionate number of crashes occur at night; (M) restricting one or more turning movements full- or part-time if alternate routes are available; (N) installing a pedestrian-actuated device (Warning Beacon, rectangular rapid-flashing beacon, or In-Roadway Warning Lights) on the major street if pedestrian safety is the major concern; (O) installing all-way stop control if the warrant is satisfied; (P) installing a pedestrian hybrid beacon (Chapter 4J) on the major street; (Q) installing a circular intersection; and (R) employing other alternatives depending on site conditions.

2B.09No Intersection Control

Guidance · Option

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01The decision not to use intersection control should be based on engineering judgment.
Option — Permissive
  1. 02The following factors may be considered: (A) intersection sight distance is adequate on all approaches; (B) all approaches are a single lane with no separate turn lanes; (C) combined motor vehicle, bicycle, and pedestrian volume (existing or projected) entering the intersection from all approaches averages less than 1,000 units per day or 80 units in the peak hour; (D) there are no marked crosswalks or bicycle lanes on any approach; (E) none of the approaches are for a through highway, main road, or higher functional classification; (F) the angle of intersection is between 90 and 75 degrees; (G) the functional classification of the intersecting streets is either two local streets, or a local street with a collector street.

2B.10Yield Control

Guidance · Support · Option · Standard

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01At intersections where a full stop is not necessary at all times, consideration should first be given to using less restrictive measures such as YIELD signs.
  2. 02Yield control should be considered when engineering judgment indicates all of the following conditions exist: (A) intersection sight distance is adequate on the YIELD-controlled approaches; (B) all approaches are a single lane with no separate turn lanes; (C) one of the following crash-related criteria applies — (D) for changing from no intersection control to yield control, two or more reported crashes in the previous 12 months susceptible to correction by a YIELD sign; (E) for changing from minor road stop control to yield control, two or fewer reported crashes in the previous 12 months; (F) combined motor vehicle, bicycle, and pedestrian volume entering the intersection averages less than 1,800 units per day or 140 units in the peak hour; (G) the angle of intersection is between 90 and 75 degrees; (H) the functional classification of the intersecting streets is either two local streets, or a local street with a collector street.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 02aRefer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for errors in Paragraph 2, Items D and E text (see Section 1A.04).
Option — Permissive
  1. 03YIELD signs may be installed at an intersection when any of the following conditions apply: (A) at the second intersection of a divided highway crossing or median break functioning as two separate intersections (see Figure 2B-19) — a YIELD sign may be installed at the entrance to the second intersection; (B) for a channelized turn lane separated from adjacent travel lanes by an island, even if adjacent lanes are signal- or STOP-controlled; (C) at an intersection with a special problem that engineering judgment indicates is susceptible to correction by a YIELD sign; (D) facing the entering roadway for a merge-type movement, if engineering judgment indicates control is needed because acceleration geometry and/or sight distance is inadequate for merging traffic; (E) on low-volume rural roads if engineering judgment indicates a YIELD sign would provide adequate control; (F) on an approach where the only permissible movement is a right turn with geometry similar to a channelized right-turn lane or a roundabout approach.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 04YIELD signs should be installed on opposing minor-street approaches (four-leg) or the minor-street approach (three-leg). When two intersecting roadways have relatively equal volumes, speeds, and other characteristics, yield control should be installed on the approach that conflicts the most with established pedestrian crossing activity, school walking routes, or bicycle crossing activity.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 05A YIELD sign shall be used to require road users to yield the right-of-way to other traffic at the entrance to a roundabout. YIELD signs at roundabouts shall be used to control the approach roadways and shall not be used to control the circulatory roadway.
  2. 06YIELD signs shall not be placed on more than one of the approaches to an intersection, except at roundabouts. Refer to CVC § 21356.

2B.11Minor Road Stop Control

Guidance

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01Stop control on the minor-road approach(es) should be considered when engineering judgment indicates one or more of: (A) a restricted view requires road users to stop to adequately observe conflicting traffic on the through street or highway; (B) crash records indicate — (1) for a four-leg intersection, three or more reported crashes in a 12-month period or six or more in a 36-month period, susceptible to correction by minor-road stop control; (2) for a three-leg intersection, three or more reported crashes in a 12-month period or five or more in a 36-month period, susceptible to correction by minor-road stop control; (C) the intersection is of a lower functional classification road with a higher functional classification road; (D) conditions that previously supported all-way stop control no longer exist.
  2. 02On low-volume rural roads, a STOP sign should be considered at an intersection where engineering judgment indicates Item C of Paragraph 1 applies, or where the intersection has inadequate sight distance for the operating vehicle speeds.

2B.12All-Way Stop Control

Support · Guidance · Option · Standard

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01The following sections describe warrants for the recommended engineering study to determine all-way stop control. Warrants are not a substitute for engineering judgment. Meeting a warrant for a particular traffic control device is not conclusive justification to install or not install all-way stop control. Because each intersection has unique characteristics affecting its operational performance or safety, the engineering study for a given intersection is ultimately the basis for the decision.
  2. 02All-way stop control at intersections with substantially differing approach volumes can reduce the effectiveness of these devices for all roadway users.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03The decision to establish all-way stop control at an unsignalized intersection should be based on an engineering study analyzing factors related to existing operation and safety, the potential to improve those conditions, and the applicable factors in the following warrants: (A) Warrant A — Crash Experience (Section 2B.13); (B) Warrant B — Sight Distance (Section 2B.14); (C) Warrant C — Transition to Signal Control or Transition to Yield Control at a Circular Intersection (Section 2B.15); (D) Warrant D — 8-Hour Volume (Vehicles, Pedestrians, Bicycles) (Section 2B.16); (E) Warrant E — Other Factors (Section 2B.17).
Option — Permissive
  1. 04The decision to install all-way stop control on site roadways open to public travel may be based on engineering judgment.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 05The satisfaction of an all-way stop control warrant or warrants shall not in itself require the installation of all-way stop control at an unsignalized intersection.

2B.13All-Way Stop Control Warrant A: Crash Experience

2B.21Speed Limit Sign (R2-1)

Support · Standard · Guidance · Option

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01In general, the maximum speed limits applicable to rural and urban roads are established: (A) Statutorily — a maximum speed limit applicable to a particular class of road, such as freeways or city streets, established by State law; or (B) As speed zones — based on engineering studies.
  2. 02State statutory limits might restrict the maximum speed limit that can be established on a particular road, notwithstanding what an engineering study might indicate.
  3. 03Agencies with designated authority to set speed limits — which include the State and, in some cases, local jurisdictions — can establish non-statutory speed limits or designate reduced speed zones using an engineering study. Setting appropriate speed limits is especially important to ensure safety for all road users in varying contexts, particularly on roadways where adjacent land use suggests that trips could be served by varied modes, such as urban and suburban non-freeway arterials or rural arterials that serve as main streets in smaller communities, consistent with the context classifications of urban core, urban, suburban, and rural towns found in AASHTO's "A Policy on Geometric Design of Highways and Streets," 2018 Edition. When setting a speed limit, factors such as land-use context, pedestrian and bicyclist activity, crash history, intersection spacing, driveway density, roadway geometry, roadside conditions, roadway functional classification, traffic volume, and observed speeds can influence the outcome; the engineering study determines which of these factors prevails.
  4. 04Jurisdictions can use speed limit setting tools and methods, such as expert systems and approaches consistent with the safe system approach, as part of the required engineering study for a non-statutory speed limit. Because such tools vary, jurisdictions need to be aware of their limitations and advantages, possible variation between tools, and gaps or weaknesses, weighing the output accordingly.
  5. 04aThe setting of speed limits can be controversial and requires a rational and defensible determination to maintain public confidence. Speed limits are normally set near the 85th-percentile speed, which statistically represents one standard deviation above the average speed and establishes the upper limit of what is considered reasonable and prudent. As with most laws, speed limits depend on the voluntary compliance of the greater majority of motorists; they cannot be set arbitrarily low, as this would create violators of the majority of drivers and would not command public respect. Artificially low speed limits can lead to poor compliance and large variations in speed within the traffic stream, and increased speed variance can create more conflicts and passing maneuvers.
  6. 05To achieve desired operating speeds, agencies often implement other speed management strategies concurrently with setting speed limits, such as traffic calming measures, geometric design features, speed safety cameras, and increased enforcement.
  7. 05aThe most effective way to reduce speeds is through a combination of strategies: traffic control devices related to speed management, roadway design and engineering solutions, traffic calming techniques, public education, and enforcement. Effectively managing road user speed relies on enforcement, roadway characteristics, surrounding environment, adjacent land use, and traffic control devices. Many studies find that engineering changes to a road's infrastructure are among the most important factors in reducing vehicle operating speeds and are also among the most effective interventions at reducing pedestrian injury and fatality rates. Potential engineering changes — curb extensions, median islands, raised crosswalks, roundabouts, and speed bumps or humps — naturally result in lower speeds, though these changes can be costly and time-consuming to implement.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 06Speed zones (other than statutory speed limits) shall only be established on the basis of an engineering and traffic survey (E&TS), also referred to as an engineering study, performed in accordance with traffic engineering practices. The engineering study shall consider the roadway context.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 07The engineering study should consider the roadway context. Factors that should be considered when conducting an engineering study for establishing or reevaluating speed limits within speed zones include: (A) Roadway environment not readily apparent to the driver (CVC § 627), such as roadside development, number and frequency of driveways and access points, land use, functional classification, public transit volume and location or frequency of stops, parking practices, and pedestrian/bicycle facilities and activity; (B) Roadway characteristics not readily apparent to the driver (CVC § 627), such as lane widths, shoulder condition, grade, alignment, median type, and sight distance; (C) Geographic context (urban district, rural town center, non-urbanized rural area, suburban area, residential district, business district, business activity district) and multi-modal trip generation; (D) Reported crash experience for at least a 36-month period; (E) Speed distribution of free-flowing vehicles, including the pace, median (50th-percentile), and 85th-percentile speeds; and (F) A review of past speed studies to identify trends in operating speeds (CVC § 22358.8).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 07aRefer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for an error in the Item E text of Paragraph 7 (see Section 1A.04).
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 08When the 85th-percentile speed is appreciably greater than the posted speed limit, and the roadway context does not support setting a higher speed limit, the engineering study (not the E&TS) should consider whether changes to geometric features, enforcement, and/or other speed-reduction countermeasures might improve compliance with the posted speed limit. A similar approach should be used if past speed studies indicate that the 85th-percentile speed has consistently increased.
  2. 09On urban and suburban arterials, and on rural arterials that serve as main streets through developed areas of communities, the 85th-percentile speed should not be used to set speed limits without consideration of all the factors described in Paragraph 7 of this Section.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 10On a freeway, expressway, or rural highway (outside urbanized locations or conditions), the speed limit posted within a speed zone shall be within 12.4 mph of the 85th-percentile speed of free-flowing motor-vehicle traffic, provided: (A) all factors described in Paragraph 7 of this Section have been considered and determined to be non-mitigating; and (B) the measures described in Paragraph 8 of this Section have been considered to the extent practicable.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 11State and local agencies should conduct engineering studies to reevaluate non-statutory speed limits on roadway segments that have undergone significant changes since the last review, such as changes to roadway context, the addition or elimination of parking or driveways, changes in the number of travel lanes, changes in the configuration of bicycle lanes, changes to road geometrics, changes in traffic signal coordination, or significant changes in traffic volumes.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 12tGenerally, the most decisive evidence of conditions not readily apparent to the driver is found in collision histories.
  2. 12uSpeed limits are established at or near the 85th-percentile speed — the speed at or below which 85 percent of traffic is moving, often called the critical speed. Speed limits higher than the 85th-percentile speed are not generally considered reasonable and prudent, while limits below the 85th percentile do not ordinarily facilitate orderly traffic movement and require constant enforcement to maintain compliance. Limits based on the 85th-percentile speed conform to the consensus of highway users as to what is reasonable and prudent, rather than the judgment of one or a few individuals.
  3. 12vThe majority of drivers comply with the basic speed law. Speed limits set at or near the 85th-percentile speed give law enforcement officers a limit to cite drivers who will not conform to what the majority considers reasonable and prudent. Studies further show that setting a speed limit below the 85th-percentile (critical) speed generally increases collision rates.

CVC § 22358.6 — 85th-Percentile Speed, Rounding, and Speed Reduction Limits

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12zWhen a speed limit is to be posted, it shall be established at the nearest 5 mph increment of the 85th-percentile speed of free-flowing traffic (CVC § 22358.6(a)), except as shown in the two Options in Paragraphs 12aa and 12ab, or if using an additional 5 mph speed reduction for a safety corridor designation (CVC § 22358.7(a)(1)) or for land/a facility generating high concentrations of bicyclists and pedestrians (CVC § 22358.7(a)(2)).
Option — Permissive
  1. 12aaWhere rounding the 85th-percentile speed to the nearest 5 mph increment would require rounding down, the speed limit may instead be reduced by 5 mph from the nearest 5 mph increment, in compliance with CVC §§ 627 and 22358.5 (CVC §§ 22353, 22353.2, 22353.3, 22353.4, and 22353.5 may also be considered, if applicable). Refer to the Standard below for documentation requirements, and to CVC § 22358.6(b).
  2. 12abWhere rounding the 85th-percentile speed to the nearest 5 mph increment would require rounding up, the speed limit may instead be rounded down to the nearest 5 mph increment below the 85th-percentile speed. Refer to CVC § 21400(b) and CVC § 22358.6(c).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12acIf a 5 mph reduction is applied to the 85th-percentile speed after it is rounded to the nearest 5 mph increment, and the required rounding was a rounding down, the E&TS shall document in writing the conditions and justification for the 5 mph reduction and be approved by a registered civil or traffic engineer. The reasons for the lower speed limit shall comply with CVC §§ 627 and 22358.5. Refer to CVC § 22358.6(b).
  2. 12adIf the 85th-percentile speed is rounded down to the lower 5 mph increment in cases where the 85th-percentile speed needed to round up to the nearest 5 mph increment, the speed limit shall not be reduced any further pursuant to CVC § 22358.6(b).
  3. 12aeThe total reduction from the 85th-percentile speed shall not exceed 12.4 mph. Refer to CVC § 22358.6(e).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 12afRefer to Table 2B-103(CA), which provides examples of 85th-percentile speed values and the application of the speed limit policies and criteria under CVC §§ 22358.6 and 22358.7.

CVC § 22358.7 — Safety Corridors and Land/Facilities Generating High Concentrations of Bicyclists or Pedestrians

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12agAdditional lowering of speed limits beyond the standards in Paragraph 12z and the Options in Paragraphs 12aa and 12ab is prohibited, except for roadway segments designated as a "safety corridor" or as "land or facilities that generate high concentrations of bicyclists and pedestrians," in compliance with CVC §§ 22358.6(d) and 22358.7.
Option — Permissive
  1. 12ahCaltrans or a local authority may additionally lower the speed limit by 5 mph from the final value determined under Paragraphs 12z, 12aa, and 12ab if, after completing an E&TS, it finds the speed limit is still more than is reasonable or safe, for either of the following reasons: (1) the portion of highway has been designated as a safety corridor; or (2) the portion of highway meets the definition of land or a facility that generates high concentrations of bicyclists or pedestrians.

"Safety Corridor" Definition — CVC § 22358.7(a)(1)

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12aiA safety corridor shall be defined as a roadway segment within an overall roadway network where the highest number of serious injury and fatality crashes occur.
  2. 12ajOne or more crash weighting factors, as provided in Paragraph 12ak, shall be used to prioritize the locations of fatal and serious injury crashes in developing the safety corridor.
Option — Permissive
  1. 12akThe crash weighting factors used to prioritize fatal and serious injury crash locations in developing a safety corridor may include, but are not limited to, the factors in Table 2B-104(CA).
  2. 12alData used to determine a safety corridor may be drawn from the most recent E&TS performed. The crash data source may include, but is not limited to, the California Highway Patrol's Statewide Integrated Traffic Records System (SWITRS).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12amThe prioritized subset of safety corridors shall: (1) identify specific locations with high crash occurrences; (2) identify corridor-level segments with a pattern of crash reoccurrence; and (3) be capable of being stratified by mode.
  2. 12anSafety corridors shall represent a prioritized subset of the overall roadway network within an authority's responsibilities and shall not exceed one-fifth of the overall roadway network.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 12aoA jurisdiction should use three to five years of the most recent crash data to determine a safety corridor based on fatal and serious injury data.
Option — Permissive
  1. 12apFor crash coverage, safety corridors may identify the subset of the overall roadway network where a minimum of 25% of the Fatal + Serious Injury (F+SI) crashes occur.
  2. 12aqTo identify logical termini, the geographic extent of a safety corridor may be determined by non-engineering staff.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12arA licensed professional engineer shall sign off on logical termini identified for a safety corridor using existing E&TS data.
Option — Permissive
  1. 12asA crash/volume rate may be used to identify additional locations to include in the safety corridor. Local agencies may use proactive measures as indicators.

"Land or Facility That Generates High Concentrations of Bicyclists or Pedestrians" Definition — CVC § 22358.7(a)(2)

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12atA land or facility that generates high concentrations of bicyclists or pedestrians shall be defined as the portion of highway where one or more of the generators listed in Table 2B-105(CA) is present within 1320 feet longitudinally of the highway, and that meets the condition specified in Paragraph 12au.
  2. 12auCrash data must demonstrate the highway segment is within the top twenty percent of pedestrian and/or bicyclist fatalities or serious injuries over a three-to-five-year period.
  3. 12avA highway segment shall be defined as the portion of highway where a location meeting the aforementioned criteria is present within 1320 feet longitudinally of the highway.
Option — Permissive
  1. 12awA highway segment may be longer than 1320 feet longitudinally provided that a minimum of one location within the top twenty percent of fatal and serious injury pedestrian and/or bicyclist crashes (within a three-to-five-year period) is present for every 1320 feet.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12axThe top twenty percent of pedestrian and/or bicyclist fatalities or serious injury crashes within a three-to-five-year period shall be based on the geographic area within the jurisdiction.
Option — Permissive
  1. 12ayA high concentration of pedestrians and bicyclists may extend longer than 1320 feet longitudinally provided a minimum of one generator is present for every 1320 feet.
  2. 12azData used to determine high-concentration locations may be obtained from the most recently performed E&TS.
  3. 12baCVC § 22358.8 (Retain Currently Adopted or Restore Immediately Prior Speed Limit) — Caltrans or a local authority may retain the currently adopted speed limit without further reduction, or restore the immediately prior adopted speed limit, on a highway that is not a freeway, without further reduction, as provided in CVC § 22358.8.

CVC § 22358.8 — Retaining or Restoring a Speed Limit

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 12bbA currently adopted speed limit or immediately prior adopted speed limit shall only be retained — by Caltrans Director's Order for the State Highway System or by local ordinance for highways under local jurisdiction — if, after completing an E&TS, Caltrans or the local authority finds the speed limit is still more than reasonable or safe; that speed limit was established with an E&TS; and a registered engineer has evaluated the highway section and determined that no additional general-purpose lanes have been added since completion of the traffic survey that established the prior speed limit.
  2. 12bcIf Caltrans or a local authority decides to use a lower speed limit based on CVC § 22358.8, after completing an E&TS and finding the speed limit is still more than reasonable or safe, it shall not be reduced by more than 5 mph from the currently adopted speed limit, nor below the immediately prior speed limit. Refer to CVC § 22358.8(b).
  3. 12bdCVC § 22358.8 shall not apply to a roadway that is a freeway.

In practice, the E&TS/speed-zoning framework in this Section is the most heavily litigated and audited part of Chapter 2B for California practitioners. The practical workflow is: (1) run the speed zone survey (100-vehicle minimum sample, off-peak/free-flow conditions) and plot the cumulative speed curve to get the 85th-percentile and pace speeds; (2) round to the nearest 5 mph increment per CVC § 22358.6(a); (3) apply the one-way-only rounding relief in 12aa/12ab; (4) only if the segment is a documented, engineer-certified safety corridor or high-bicyclist/pedestrian-generator segment, apply the single additional 5 mph reduction under § 22358.7 — the 12.4 mph total cap (Paragraph 12ae) is an absolute ceiling that cannot be exceeded by stacking justifications. Option — Permissive
  1. 04Reversing traffic flow may be controlled with pavement markings and Reversible Lane Control signs (without lane-use control signals) when all of the following conditions are met: (A) only one lane is being reversed; (B) an engineering study indicates that Reversible Lane Control signs alone would result in an acceptable level of safety and efficiency; and (C) there are no unusual or complex operations in the reversible lane pattern.

  1. 05Reversible Lane Control signs shall contain the legend or symbols designating the allowable uses of the lane and the time periods such uses are allowed. Where symbols and legends are used, their meanings shall be as shown in Table 2B-2.
  2. 06Reversible Lane Control signs shall consist of a white background with a black legend and border, except for the R3-9e sign, where the color red is used for the X symbol.
  3. 07Symbol signs, such as the R3-9e sign, shall consist of the appropriate symbol in the upper portion of the sign with the applicable times of day and days of the week below it. All times of day and days of the week shall be accounted for on the sign to eliminate confusion to the road user.
  4. 08In situations where more than one message is conveyed to the road user, such as on the R3-9e sign, the sign legend shall be arranged as follows: (A) the prohibition or restriction message is the primary legend and shall be on top for word message signs and to the far left for symbol signs; (B) the permissive use message shall be displayed as the second legend; and (C) the OTHER TIMES message shall be displayed at the bottom for word message signs and to the far right for symbol signs.

  1. 09Symbol signs may also include a downward-pointing arrow with the legend THIS LANE. The term OTHER TIMES may be used for either the symbol or word message sign.

  1. 10A Reversible Lane Control sign shall be mounted over the approximate center of the lane being reversed.

  2. 18If used, overhead signs should be located at intervals not greater than ¼ mile. The bottom of overhead Reversible Lane Control signs should not be more than 19 feet above the pavement grade.

2B.35Jughandle Signs (R3-23, R3-24, R3-25, and R3-26 Series)

Support · Standard · Option

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01A jughandle turn is a left turn or U-turn that, because of special geometry, is made by initially making a right turn. This type of turn can increase the operational efficiency of a roadway by eliminating the need for mandatory left-turn lanes, and can increase the efficiency of a traffic control signal by eliminating the need for protected left-turn phases. A jughandle turn can also provide an opportunity for trucks and commercial vehicles to make a U-turn where the median and roadway are not wide enough to accommodate a traditional U-turn by these vehicles.
  2. 02Figure 2B-8 shows the various signs that can be used for signing jughandle turns. Figure 2B-9 shows examples of regulatory and destination guide signing for various types of jughandle turns.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 03On multi-lane roadways, since road users generally anticipate needing to be in the left-hand lane when approaching a location where they desire to turn left or make a U-turn, an ALL TURNS FROM RIGHT LANE (R3-23) or a U TURN FROM RIGHT LANE (R3-23a) sign (see Figure 2B-8) shall be installed in advance of the location to inform drivers that left turns and/or U-turns will be made from the right-hand lane.
Option — Permissive
  1. 04Where a median of sufficient width is available, supplemental regulatory or guide signs may also be placed on the left-hand side of the roadway.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 05The R3-24 series sign, with an upward diagonal arrow pointing to the right, if the jughandle entrance is designed as an exit ramp (see Drawings A and B in Figure 2B-9), or the R3-25 series sign, with a horizontal arrow pointing to the right, if the jughandle entrance is designed as an intersection, shall be installed on the right-hand side of the roadway at the entrance to the jughandle. The legend on the sign shall be ALL TURNS, U TURN, or U AND LEFT TURNS, as appropriate.
  2. 06If the jughandle is designed such that the jughandle entrance is downstream of the location where the turn would normally have been made (see Drawing C in Figure 2B-9), the R3-26 series sign, with an arrow pointing straight upward, shall be installed on the right-hand side of the roadway at the intersection to inform road users that they need to proceed straight through the intersection to make a left turn or U-turn. The legend on the sign shall be U TURN or U AND LEFT TURNS, as appropriate.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 07The R3-24, R3-25, and R3-26 series signs are designed to be mounted below conventional guide signs.
  2. 08Section 2C.12 contains information regarding the use of advisory exit and ramp speed signs for exit ramps.
  3. 09Section 2D.40 contains information regarding the use of guide signs for jughandles.

In practice, the choice among the R3-24 (Drawing A), R3-25 (Drawing B), and R3-26 (Drawing C) series hinges entirely on where the jughandle physically diverges relative to the main intersection: upstream and ramp-like (R3-24), upstream and at-grade (R3-25), or downstream of where the turn would conventionally be made (R3-26, requiring drivers to first go straight through the main intersection). Getting the right series matched to the geometry is what prevents the classic jughandle wrong-turn mistake by unfamiliar drivers.

2B.52Parking, Standing, and Stopping Signs (R7 and R8 Series)

Support · Option · Guidance · Standard

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Parking signs pertain to the parking, standing, and stopping of vehicles along the roadway and in designated parking areas. They cover a wide variety of regulations, and only general guidance can be provided here. The word "standing," when used on the R7 and R8 series of signs, refers to the practice of a driver keeping the vehicle in a stationary position while continuing to occupy the vehicle. The word "stopping," when used on the R7 and R8 series signs, refers to any vehicle, occupied by a driver or not, that stops other than to avoid conflict with other traffic or to comply with official direction. Other types of activities such as active loading, active passenger loading, and/or waiting might be established in State or local codes for use on R7 and R8 series parking, standing, and stopping signs.
  2. 01aCVC Section 463 defines "parking," which involves the standing of a vehicle, whether occupied or not; however, the temporary loading or unloading of merchandise or passengers is not considered parking. CVC Section 587 defines "stopping," which involves the cessation of vehicle movement.
  3. 01bRefer to CVC 21112 through 22658 regarding the authorities for the various parking, stopping, and loading signs.
  4. 02Parking signs are categorized as either (1) prohibiting parking or (2) permitting parking with restrictions on how parking is allowed.
  5. 03The types of parking, standing, or stopping prohibitions that might be encountered include, but are not limited to: (A) Prohibited at all times; (B) Prohibited only at certain times of the day and/or days of the week; (C) Prohibited with exceptions, such as for bus stops, loading/unloading zones, persons with disabilities, or electric vehicle charging stations; or (D) Prohibited under certain conditions, such as Snow Emergency Routes.
  6. 04Permissive parking signs allowing parking with restrictions include, but are not limited to: (A) Parking only allowed for limited time duration (such as 30 minutes or 1 hour); (B) Metered parking requiring payment at an individual or a multi-space parking meter, or through electronic means such as by telephone or mobile application; (C) Parking only for specific persons (such as those with disabilities, or patrons or employees of a business) or specific vehicle types (such as electric vehicles, police/government vehicles, motorcycles, bicycles, or taxis); (D) Angled or back-in angled parking when it is not commonly used in the area; (E) Parking programs such as neighborhood/residential permits, school areas, or special events; and (F) Emergency parking or stopping only.
  7. 04aRefer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for an error in the Paragraph 4, Item B text. Refer to Section 1A.04 for more details.

2B.54Placement of Parking, Standing, and Stopping Signs

Support · Guidance · Option

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01The efficacy of parking, standing, and stopping signs, when used on conventional roads in urbanized or developed environments, depends on their visibility and consistent placement along a street or within a particular block. It is often impracticable for the entire legend to be legible from the same distances as other types of signs. 2B.76Gates

Support · Standard · Option · Guidance

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Gates described in this Section, used for weather or other emergency conditions, are typically permanently installed to enable the gate to be immediately deployed as needed to prohibit the entry of traffic to the highway segment(s).
  2. 02A gate typically features a gate arm that is moved from a vertical to a horizontal position, or is rotated in a horizontal plane from parallel to traffic to perpendicular to traffic. Traffic is obstructed and required to stop when the gate arm is placed in a horizontal position perpendicular to traffic. Another type of gate consists of a segment of fence (usually on rollers) that swings open and closed, or that is retracted to open and extended to close.
  3. 03Gates are sometimes used to enforce a required stop. Examples of such uses include: (A) parking facility entrances and exits; (B) private community entrances and exits; (C) military base entrances and exits; (D) toll plaza lanes; (E) movable bridges (see Chapter 4Q); (F) Automated Flagger Assistance Devices (see Chapter 6L); and (G) grade crossings (see Part 8).
  4. 04Gates are sometimes used to periodically close a roadway or a ramp. Examples of such uses include: (A) closing ramps to implement counter-flow operations for evacuations; (B) closing ramps that lead to reversible lanes; and (C) closing roadways for weather events such as snow, ice, or flooding, or for other emergencies.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 05Except as provided in Paragraph 6, gate arms, if used, shall be fully retroreflective on both sides, with vertical stripes alternately red and white at 16-inch intervals measured horizontally, as shown in Figure 8D-1. The width (which becomes the height of the retroreflective sheeting when the gate is in the down position) of the retroreflective sheeting on the front of the gate arm shall be at least 4 inches. Refer to Section 8D.03, Paragraph 5, if the gate arm extends beyond 32 feet.
Option — Permissive
  1. 06If used on a one-way roadway or ramp, the retroreflective sheeting may be omitted on the side of the gate (or rolling fence) facing away from approaching traffic.
  2. 07Where gate arms are used to block off ramps into reversible lanes or to redirect approaching traffic, the red and white striping may be angled such that the stripes slope downward at an angle of 45 degrees toward the side of the gate arm on which traffic is to pass.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 08The gate arm shall extend across the approaching lane or lanes of traffic to effectively block motor vehicle, bicycle, and/or pedestrian travel as appropriate.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 09When a gate that is rotated in a horizontal plane is in the position where it is parallel to traffic (indicating that the roadway is open), the outer end of the gate arm should be rotated to the downstream direction (from the perspective of traffic in the lane adjacent to the gate support) to prevent spearing if the gate is struck by an errant vehicle.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 10If red lights are attached to a traffic gate, the red lights shall be steadily illuminated or flashed only during the period when the gate is in the horizontal or closed position and when the gate is in the process of being opened or closed.
  2. 11Except as provided in Paragraph 6, rolling sections of fence, if used, shall include either a horizontal strip of retroreflective sheeting on both sides of the fence with vertical stripes alternately red and white at 16-inch intervals measured horizontally to simulate the appearance of a gate arm in the horizontal position, or one or more Type 4 object markers (see Section 2C.73), or both. If a horizontal strip of retroreflective sheeting is used, the bottom of the sheeting shall be located 3.5 to 4.5 feet above the roadway surface.

Like barricades, gates have no dedicated figure in this chapter; the red-and-white 16-inch striping pattern for gate arms is shown in Figure 8D-1 (Part 8).

★ Key Takeaways — Chapter 2B

  • STOP and YIELD signs are shape-exclusive and highly regulated in placement: near-side, right-hand approach, never on the same post as each other, and governed by a strict hierarchy of intersection control (no control → yield control → minor-road stop → all-way stop) that must be justified by engineering judgment or the five all-way-stop warrants (crash experience, sight distance, transition, 8-hour volume, other factors) — meeting a warrant never mandates installation.
  • Section 2B.21 (Speed Limit Sign) is the legal core of the chapter for California practice: non-statutory speed limits require an Engineering and Traffic Survey (E&TS); posted limits are set at the nearest 5 mph increment of the 85th-percentile speed with a hard 12.4 mph total-reduction ceiling (CVC § 22358.6(e)); the one-time-only additional 5 mph reduction under § 22358.7 is available solely for documented, engineer-certified "safety corridors" or "high bicyclist/pedestrian concentration" segments; and an E&TS is valid 5–14 years depending on enforcement method, which is what shields a jurisdiction from a "speed trap" challenge under CVC § 40802.
  • Movement and lane control signs (2B.26–2B.35) distinguish Movement Prohibition signs (whole-approach turn bans, R3-1 through R3-4/R3-18/R3-27) from Intersection Lane Control signs (per-lane requirements/options, R3-5 through R3-8 series) — the two families are never substitutes for each other, and R73(CA)/R61(CA) series signs are the California-specific tool for regulating U-turns per signal phase at protected-left-turn intersections instead of a blanket No U-Turn sign.
  • DO NOT ENTER, WRONG WAY, and ONE WAY signing at interchange ramps (2B.46–2B.50) follows prescribed sign packages and mounting heights (lower than standard, to be most responsive to headlights) specifically to combat wrong-way freeway entries.
  • Parking, Standing, and Stopping signs (2B.52–2B.55) are keyed to curb-zone color, with red for prohibitive, green for permissive, blue for accessible parking (R7-8/R99(CA)), and a fixed top-to-bottom legend order (tow-away notice, restriction, times, days, qualifiers, exemptions, municipal code, impound phone number).
  • Weight Limit signs (2B.64) fix vehicle-symbol meanings by configuration (single-unit, single-trailer, multi-trailer) regardless of actual axle count or shape, and require an advance sign so restricted vehicles can detour before reaching the limit zone.
  • The ten California-only sections (2B.101(CA)–2B.110(CA)) cover locally-adopted-ordinance signs (fishing/jumping from bridges, littering and animal-abandonment fines, private-road vehicle-code enforcement, golf carts, emergency-access keep-clear, off-highway-vehicle routes, no-camping, begin/end freeway, and no-loitering/vending signs) — most require an underlying local resolution or ordinance before the sign may legally be posted or enforced.
