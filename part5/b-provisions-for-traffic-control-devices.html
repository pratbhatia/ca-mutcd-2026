Part 5 · Traffic Control Device Considerations for Automated Vehicles · Chapter 5B

Provisions for Traffic Control Devices

Device-category-specific guidance — signs, markings, highway traffic signals, temporary traffic control, highway-rail/light rail grade crossings, and bicycle facilities — for agencies that want to better accommodate driving automation systems while also benefitting human road users.

5B.01Signs

Support · Standard · Guidance.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01
    Driving automation systems use sensors, algorithms, and processing to locate, read, and comprehend traffic signs and assist the human driver or AV in appropriately making vehicle operational decisions. Location, condition, uniformity, design characteristics, and consistent application all affect the ability of driving automation systems to perform these functions.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 02
    When scanning graphics (see Section 2A.04) of any type are used on a sign for support of driving automation systems, the scanning graphics shall not be visible to the human eye and the sign shall have no apparent loss of resolution or recognition for the road user.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03
    Agencies seeking to better accommodate driving automation systems to support AVs, while also potentially benefitting human drivers, should consider:
    • A. Clearly associating the sign location and application with the displayed message to the specific lane or road to which it applies, such as in the case of parallel roads or lanes with different speed limits or restrictions.
    • B. The practice of sign and information spreading (see Section 2A.20) to limit the amount of information displayed in one location or on one sign to minimize sign clutter.
    • C. Signs with designs that are otherwise not provided for in this Manual or the "Standard Highway Signs" publication (see Section 1A.05) being designed based on the standardized sign design practices and features as provided for in this Manual for the type of sign, the location, and the characteristics of the roadway on which it is used.
    • D. The refresh rate of LEDs in the illuminated portion of electronic-display signs to provide for greater consistency in driving automation system detection.

In practice, Paragraph 02 is the only Standard-level (mandatory) requirement in all of Part 5: if an agency does use scanning graphics of any kind on a sign face to aid machine reading, those graphics must be invisible to the human eye and must not degrade the sign's legibility or resolution for ordinary road users — this protects the human-driver experience even where a sign has been augmented for AV use.

5B.02Markings

Support · Guidance — no Standard or Option statements in this Section.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01
    Driving automation systems use sensors, algorithms, and processing to locate, read, and comprehend pavement markings. Location, condition, uniformity, design characteristics, and consistent application all have some effect on the ability of driving automation systems to perform this function. Certain pavement marking applications and practices have been shown through research to better support driving automation system technology, while also benefitting, or at least not detracting from, the performance of the human operator.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 02
    Agencies seeking to better accommodate driving automation system to support AVs, while also potentially benefitting human drivers, should consider:
    • A. Normal width longitudinal lines of at least 6 inches in width (see Section 3A.04).
    • B. Edge lines of at least 6 inches in width (see Sections 3A.04 and 3B.09).
    • C. Dotted edge line extensions along all entrance and exit ramps, all auxiliary lanes, and all tapers where a deceleration or auxiliary lane is added (see Section 3B.11).
    • D. Chevron markings in the neutral areas of exit gores to distinguish them from travel lanes (see Section 3B.25).
    • E. Raised pavement markers only as a supplement to, rather than as a substitute for, pavement markings (see Sections 3B.16 and 3B.17).
    • F. Uniform contrast markings on light-colored pavements to create greater contrast (see Section 3A.03).
    • G. Broken lines with uniform marking and gap length (see Section 3A.04).

In practice, every item in this list is a marking practice already permitted or described elsewhere in Parts 3A/3B — nothing here creates a new marking type. The AV-oriented value is in consistently choosing the wider (6-inch-plus), higher-contrast, and more continuous end of the existing range of MUTCD-compliant options, particularly at gores, ramps, and auxiliary lane tapers where automation systems most often struggle with lane-boundary interpretation.

5B.03Highway Traffic Signals

Guidance · Support — no Standard or Option statements in this Section.

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01
    Agencies seeking to better accommodate driving automation systems to support AVs, while also potentially benefitting human drivers, should consider:
    • A. Consistent signal face placement along corridors with respect to overhead mounting versus post mounting on the side of the roadway (see Sections 4D.05 thru 4D.10).
    • B. Consistent number of signal faces for approach lanes and the selection of signal indications and signal clusters along a corridor to promote uniform displays for identical or similar situations (see Sections 4D.05 and 4D.06).
    • C. The refresh rate of LED traffic signals to provide for greater consistency in driving automation system detection.
    • D. Providing signal faces with backplates (see Section 4D.06) having retroreflective borders to enhance signal face conspicuity and detection by driving automation system sensors.
    • E. Using FLASHING YELLOW ARROW signal indications for permissive turns.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 02
    Signal faces that display a CIRCULAR GREEN indication and that are located over or directly in line with a mandatory turn lane can be less effective for driving automation systems to recognize as a traffic signal face controlling permissive turning movements.
  2. 03
    Achieving uniformity along a corridor is desirable for driving automation systems, but can be challenging. Multiple options are available for traffic signal displays to allow design variations based on specific intersection variables such as available overhead clearance, utility conflicts, signal support design constraints, and other factors. V2I capabilities can complement driving automation system recognition of traffic signals to provide redundancy, and to improve reliability and accuracy.

In practice, Paragraph 02 explains why Item E (favoring FLASHING YELLOW ARROW for permissive turns) matters: a CIRCULAR GREEN indication aligned with a mandatory turn lane is ambiguous to a machine-vision system in a way a human driver resolves easily from lane context and habit, whereas a flashing yellow arrow signals "permissive turn" unambiguously regardless of lane geometry.

5B.04Temporary Traffic Control

Guidance · Support — no Standard or Option statements in this Section.

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01
    Agencies seeking to better accommodate driving automation systems to support, while also potentially benefitting human drivers, in and through temporary traffic control (TTC) zones should consider:
    • A. Consistent type, spacing, and mounting height of signs (see Sections 6B.04 and 6F.02).
    • B. Use of the END ROAD WORK (G20-2) sign to establish the end of the TTC zone (see Section 6H.36).
    • C. Wider retroreflective material on, or reduced spacing of, channelizing devices to better accommodate driving automation system sensors in nighttime and adverse weather conditions (see Chapter 6K).
    • D. Continuous markings at the beginning of TTC zones and in lane transitions.
    • E. Temporary raised pavement markers only as a supplement to, rather than as a substitute for, pavement markings (see Section 6J.03).
    • F. Removal or obliteration of pavement markings that are no longer applicable as soon as practicable, for long-term stationary operations in the temporary traveled way (see Section 6J.01).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01a
    Refer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for error in Paragraph 1 text. Refer to Section 1A.04 for more details.
  2. 02
    Pavement markings that are not fully removed and pavement scarring are of particular concern as there can be misinterpretation by driving automation systems that can result in erroneous vehicle positioning in TTC zones.
  3. 03
    V2I communications can complement driving automation systems recognition in TTC zones by communicating the presence of a TTC zone to vehicles.
  4. 04
    Section 6J.01 describes the use of pavement markings in TTC zones and the removal or obliteration of existing pavement markings.
  5. 05
    Section 6J.02 describes the use of temporary pavement markings in TTC zones.

In practice, Paragraph 01a is an official erratum notice as published in the source, not a substantive requirement — it flags that FHWA's List of Known Errors documents a known error in the Guidance Paragraph 01 text of this Section and directs readers to Section 1A.04 for how the Manual handles known errors. Engineers relying on this Section for TTC design should independently check FHWA's current List of Known Errors for the specific correction before applying Paragraph 01 verbatim.

5B.05Traffic Control for Highway-Rail and Highway-Light Rail Transit Grade Crossings

Guidance · Support — no Standard or Option statements in this Section.

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01
    Agencies seeking to better accommodate driving automation systems to support AVs, while also potentially benefitting human drivers, at grade crossings should consider:
    • A. Consistent placement of signs and markings for passive and active grade crossings along a corridor to promote uniformity and to improve the ability of driving automation system technology to recognize grade crossings.
    • B. Removal of signs and pavement markings associated with grade crossings that are out of service (see Section 8A.09).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 02
    V2I communications can complement driving automation system recognition of grade crossings to improve reliability and accuracy, and to relay information on the arrival or presence of a train or LRT vehicle at a grade crossing.

In practice, this Section calls back directly to Chapter 5A.04's Item F concern (deciphering traffic control at highway-rail or highway-LRT grade crossings, especially passive crossings) — passive crossings, which rely entirely on static signs and markings with no active warning device, are the case where corridor-wide consistency and prompt removal of out-of-service devices matter most for automation system recognition.

5B.06Traffic Control for Bicycle Facilities

Guidance · Support — no Standard or Option statements in this Section.

Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 01
    Agencies seeking to better accommodate driving automation systems to support AVs, while also potentially benefitting human road users, should consider:
    • A. Use of an END (R3-9dP) plaque with a BIKE LANE (R3-17) sign to indicate the end of a bicycle lane that is merging with other traffic (see Sections 2B.33 and 9B.04).
    • B. Use of Bicycle Lane Ends (W9-5) and Bicycle Merging (W9-5a) warning signs in advance of the end of a bicycle lane and where a merging maneuver might occur (see Section 9C.07).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 02
    Bicycle facilities that are physically separated from motor vehicle traffic using vertical objects or vertical separation can facilitate detection from driving automation system sensors (see Section 9E.07).

In practice, the R3-9dP END plaque, R3-17 BIKE LANE sign, and W9-5/W9-5a warning signs referenced here are standard bicycle facility devices already defined in Parts 2B and 9C/9B — this Section is simply flagging that clear advance notice of a bicycle lane's end is a merge-conflict scenario where both AVs and human drivers benefit from the same well-marked, standardized warning.

★ Key Takeaways — Chapter 5B

  • Part 5's single Standard-level requirement is in Section 5B.01, Paragraph 02: sign scanning graphics used to aid driving automation systems shall not be visible to the human eye and shall cause no apparent loss of resolution or recognition for the road user. Every other provision in Chapter 5B is Guidance or Support.
  • For markings (5B.02), the AV-supportive practices are all within the existing MUTCD toolbox: 6-inch-plus longitudinal and edge lines, dotted edge-line extensions at ramps/auxiliary lanes/tapers, exit-gore chevrons, raised pavement markers used only as supplements, contrast markings on light pavement, and uniform broken-line dash/gap length.
  • For signals (5B.03), corridor-wide consistency in mounting (overhead vs. post), signal-face counts, LED refresh rate, and retroreflective-bordered backplates all aid automation-system detection; FLASHING YELLOW ARROW is preferred over CIRCULAR GREEN for permissive turns because a green ball over a mandatory-turn lane can be misread by machine vision.
  • For temporary traffic control (5B.04), full and prompt removal/obliteration of obsolete markings is critical — leftover markings or pavement scarring can cause an automation system to erroneously position a vehicle in a TTC zone; note the flagged erratum in Paragraph 01a referencing FHWA's List of Known Errors for Paragraph 01.
  • For grade crossings (5B.05) and bicycle facilities (5B.06), consistent corridor-wide device placement, prompt removal of out-of-service devices, and standard advance-warning signage for bicycle lane ends all reduce ambiguity for both automation systems and human road users.
  • V2I (vehicle-to-infrastructure) communication is called out repeatedly across Sections 5B.03–5B.05 as a complementary, redundant channel — it improves reliability and accuracy of automation-system recognition but does not replace the underlying physical traffic control device.
