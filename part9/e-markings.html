Markings

The complete set of pavement marking treatments for California's four bikeway classes — bicycle lane lines, symbols, and arrows; buffer-separated and physically separated bicycle lanes; intersection extensions and mixing zones; shared-lane markings; two-stage turn boxes and bicycle boxes; shared-use path markings; and green-colored pavement — with the dimensions, colors, and conditions of use that govern each.

9E.01Bicycle Lanes (Class II Bikeways (Bike Lane or Buffer-Separated Bicycle Lane))

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Pavement markings designate that portion of the roadway for preferential use by bicyclists. Markings inform all road users of the restricted nature of the bicycle lane.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 02Longitudinal pavement markings and bicycle lane symbol or word markings (see Figure 9E-1) shall be used to define bicycle lanes.
  2. 02aThe BIKE LANE pavement markings shall be placed on the far side of each intersection.
Option — Permissive
  1. 02bThe BIKE LANE pavement markings may also be placed at other locations as desired.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 02cExamples of BIKE LANE pavement markings are shown in various figures in this chapter.
Option — Permissive
  1. 02dOptional word, arrow, and symbol markings with details as shown in Figure 9E-1 may be used.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03The first symbol, or word, and/or green-colored pavement marking in a bicycle lane should be placed at the beginning of the bicycle lane, and downstream symbol or word markings should be placed after major intersections. Additional symbol or word markings should be placed at periodic intervals along the bicycle lane based on engineering judgment. Refer to Figure 9E-109(CA).
  2. 03aBicycle lane markings on Class II Bikeways should be placed a constant distance from the marked lane line or centerline, as appropriate. Bike lanes with parking permitted should not be directed toward the curb at intersections or localized areas where parking is prohibited, since that practice prevents bicyclists from following a straight course. Where transitions from one type of bike lane to another are necessary, smooth tapers should be provided.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 03bClass II Bikeways require standard signing and pavement markings as shown in Figure 3A-112(CA). Bike lane lines are not typically extended through intersections.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03cWhere right turns are not permitted, the solid bike lane stripe should extend to the edge of the intersection and begin again on the far side. Where there is no right-turn-only lane, but right turns are permitted, the solid stripe should terminate 50 feet to 200 feet prior to the intersection.
Option — Permissive
  1. 03dA dashed line, as shown in Figure 3A-112(CA), may be carried to, or near, the intersection. Where city blocks are short (less than 400 feet), the length of dashed stripe may be 50 feet.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03eWhere blocks are longer or vehicle speeds are high (greater than 35 mph), the length of dashed stripe should be increased to 200 feet.
  2. 03fMarkings for a bike lane where vehicle parking is prohibited or permitted are shown in Figures 9E-2, 9E-2(CA), 9E-6(CA), 9E-101(CA), 9E-103(CA), and 9E-106(CA).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 03gWhere motorist right turns are permitted, the solid bike lane shall either be dropped entirely, or dashed beginning at a point between 50 feet and 200 feet in advance of the intersection.
Option — Permissive
  1. 03hIn areas where parking stalls are not necessary (because parking is light), a 4- to 6-inch-wide solid white stripe on local agency roadways and a 6-inch-wide stripe on state highways may be painted to fully delineate the bike lane. This may be advisable where there is concern that motorists may misconstrue the bike lane to be a traffic lane.
  2. 04An arrow marking (see Figure 9E-1) may be used in conjunction with the bicycle lane symbol or word marking, placed downstream from the symbol or word marking.
  3. 05Where the bicycle lane symbols or word markings are used, Bicycle Lane signs (see Section 9B.04) may also be used, but not necessarily adjacent to every set of pavement markings, in order to avoid overuse of the signs.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 06Section 3H.06 contains information on green-colored pavement for use in bicycle lanes. Refer to Figure 9E-109(CA).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 06aRaised barriers (e.g., raised traffic bars and asphalt concrete dikes) or raised pavement markers shall not be used to delineate bike lanes on Class II Bikeways.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 06bRaised barriers prevent motorists from merging into bike lanes before making right turns, as required by the CVC, and restrict the movement of bicyclists desiring to enter or exit bike lanes.
  2. 06cThey also impede routine maintenance. Raised pavement markers increase the difficulty for bicyclists when entering or exiting bike lanes, and discourage motorists from merging into bike lanes before making right turns.
Option — Permissive
  1. 06dPhysical barriers or other vertical elements may be used to convert a Class II Bikeway to a Class I Bikeway (Bike Path) or Class IV Bikeway (Cycle Track, Separated Bikeway or Separated Bicycle Lane).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 07The bicycle symbol or BIKE LANE pavement word marking and the pavement marking arrow shall not be used in a shoulder.
  2. 08A portion of the roadway shall not be established as both a shoulder and a bicycle lane.
Option — Permissive
  1. 08aThe BIKE LANE may count towards the shoulder width per Caltrans Highway Design Manual Index 301.2.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 09Where a shoulder is provided or is of sufficient width to meet the expectation of a highway user in that it can function as a space for emergency, enforcement, or maintenance activities, or avoidance or recovery maneuvers, Section 9B.16 contains information regarding the Bicycles Use Shoulder Only sign that can be used to denote locations on a freeway or expressway where bicycles are permitted on an available and usable shoulder.
  2. 10Examples of pavement markings for bicycle lanes on a two-way street are shown in Figure 9E-2 and 9E-2(CA).

In practice, the 50-to-200-foot dashed-stripe rule is one of the most frequently misapplied dimensions in bike lane design — remember the trigger is the 35 mph threshold (200 feet at or above, 50 feet below), not block length alone, and short blocks under 400 feet allow the 50-foot minimum regardless of speed.

9E.02Bicycle Lanes at Intersection Approaches (Class II Bikeways)

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 01Except as provided in Paragraph 2 of this Section, a through bicycle lane shall not be positioned to the right of a right-turn-only lane or to the left of a left-turn-only lane.
Option — Permissive
  1. 02A through bicycle lane may be positioned to the right of a right-turn-only lane or to the left of a left-turn-only lane provided that the bicycle lane is controlled by a traffic signal that displays bicycle signal indications (see Chapter 4H).
  2. 02aA bicycle lane for travel in the same direction as the general-purpose lanes may be placed on the left-hand side of the general-purpose lanes.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 03Unless controlled by a bicycle signal indication, a bicyclist continuing straight through an intersection from the right of a right-turn-only lane or from the left of a left-turn-only lane would be inconsistent with normal traffic behavior and would violate the expectations of right-turning or left-turning motorists.
Option — Permissive
  1. 03aWhen a bike lane approaches an intersection with right- or left-turn-only lanes, Figure 9E-3 and Figure 9E-3(CA) may be used.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03bWhere through motor vehicle lanes approaching an intersection become mandatory turn lanes, adjacent bike lanes should be delineated using Figure 9E-3(CA).
  2. 04When the right (left) through lane is dropped to become a mandatory right-turn (left-turn) lane, the bicycle lane markings should stop at least 100 feet before the beginning of the right-turn (left-turn) lane. Through bicycle lane markings should resume to the left (right) of the mandatory right-turn (left-turn) lane.
  3. 05Except as provided in Paragraph 2 of this Section, an optional through-right (through-left) turn lane next to a mandatory right-turn (left-turn) lane should not be used where there is a through bicycle lane.
  4. 05aA dashed line across the right-turn-only lane should not be used on extremely long lanes, or where there are double right-turn-only lanes. For these types of intersections, all striping should be dropped to permit judgment by the bicyclists to prevail.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 06A bicycle lane located on an intersection approach between general-purpose lanes for motor vehicle movements shall be marked with at least one bicycle symbol and at least one arrow pavement marking as provided in Paragraph 4 of Section 9E.01.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 06aRefer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for an error in the Paragraph 6 text. Refer to Section 1A.04 for more details.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 07A bicycle lane shall not be marked within a general-purpose lane, either with dotted or any other line markings.
Option — Permissive
  1. 08Where there is insufficient width in the roadway to include both a bicycle lane and a general-purpose turn lane, bicycle travel may be accommodated within the turn lane or general-purpose lane using shared-lane markings.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 09Where a general-purpose turn lane is controlled by a traffic control signal, through bicycle movements shall not be accommodated in the turn lane unless the turning movement is always permitted to proceed simultaneously with the adjacent through movement.
Option — Permissive
  1. 09aA Bicycle Crossing (W11-1) sign may be used to warn road users of the potential for bicyclists crossing their path. Refer to Section 9B.18.
  2. 09bWhen a bike lane approaches a ramp intersection that intersects the local facility at or close to 90 degrees (typical of a compact or spread diamond configuration), then Figure 9E-3 and Figure 9E-3(CA) may be used.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 09cHowever, when a bike lane approaches one or more ramp intersections that intersect the local facility at various angles other than 90 degrees (typically high-speed, skewed ramps), Figure 9E-102(CA) should be used.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 10Examples of bicycle lane markings on approaches to intersections are shown in Figures 9E-3, 9E-3(CA), 9E-4, and 9E-9.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 11The longitudinal line defining a bicycle lane should be dotted on approaches to intersections where turning vehicles are permitted to cross the path of through-moving bicycles (see Figure 9D-7).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 11aIf used, the space for bicycle use shall be delineated by Detail 39 on the right of the through lane and Detail 38A on the left of the right-turn-only lane. Refer to Figure 3A-112(CA).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 12Buffer-separated and separated bicycle lanes require additional considerations at intersections, including sight distances for bicycles and other road users, user expectations, and intersection geometry.
Option — Permissive
  1. 13A buffer-separated or separated bicycle lane may be shifted closer to, or farther away from, the adjacent general-purpose lane depending upon site-specific conditions (see Drawings D and E in Figure 9E-7).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 14A buffer-separated or separated bicycle lane shifted away from the adjacent general-purpose lane at an intersection can create space for a motor vehicle to queue between the general-purpose lane and the extension of the bicycle lane. This design can also improve the safety and comfort of bicyclists by reducing the speed of turning motor vehicles, improving sightlines, and creating additional buffer space prior to the conflict point with turning motor vehicles.
  2. 15The purpose of shifting a buffer-separated or separated bicycle lane away from the adjacent general-purpose lane is to allow the driver of a turning vehicle to undertake the tasks of turning and scanning for bicycle cross traffic in isolation versus simultaneously. Sufficient sight distance for both drivers and bicyclists is important in this design (see Drawing E in Figure 9E-7).
  3. 16The purpose of shifting a buffer-separated or separated bicycle lane toward the adjacent general-purpose lane is to improve the visibility of bicyclists to the adjacent traffic and avoid conflicts between turning motor vehicles and bicyclists (see Drawing D in Figure 9E-7).
Option — Permissive
  1. 16aAt locations with right-turn-only lanes where bicycles are not prohibited but Class II bicycle facilities do not exist on the approach, a minimum 4-foot-wide space for bicycle use may be provided between the right-turn and through lane; where the posted speed is greater than 40 mph the minimum width should be 6 feet.
  2. 16bWhen the width between the right-turn and through lane is greater than 4 feet, a buffer area may be striped adjacent to the 4-foot minimum width for bicycle travel, regardless of the posted speed.
  3. 16cThe buffer may be placed on the left or on the right of the 4-foot space for bicycle travel.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 16dRefer to Caltrans' Highway Design Manual, Index 403.6, Turning Traffic.
  2. 16eRefer to Figure 9E-3 and Figure 9E-3(CA) for details on striping and Figure 9E-6 and Figure 9E-6(CA) for details on buffer area striping.
  3. 17Staggering stop lines (see Section 3B.19) so that general-purpose lanes stop further in advance from the intersection than the bicycle lane can improve the visibility of bicyclists for drivers of turning vehicles (see Drawing D in Figure 9E-7).
Option — Permissive
  1. 18Where a general-purpose mandatory turn lane is provided at an intersection and the approach also includes a separated or buffer-separated bicycle lane, a mixing zone may be established to allow general-purpose turning traffic to share the roadway space with bicyclists (see Figure 9E-5).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 19Mixing zones shall be used only where the bicycle lane is one-way in the same direction of travel as the adjacent general-purpose lane.
  2. 20Mixing zones with a yielding area shall have yield markings indicating where general-purpose traffic entering the shared space shall yield to bicyclists.
  3. 21Where a mixing zone continues to the intersection itself, sharing space between bicyclists and general-purpose turning traffic, shared-lane markings and turn arrows shall be provided in the lane.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 22Mixing zones require bicycles and general traffic to share space, interrupting a buffer-separated or separated bicycle lane where bicycle traffic is otherwise separated from general traffic. The preference is to provide a dedicated bicycle facility for the intersection approach; if that is not possible, the mixing zone needs to indicate that bicyclists and motorists are entering a shared condition.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 23Where a mixing zone provides for the re-establishment of a bicycle lane after bicycles and general-purpose lanes cross paths, a buffered or physically separated space should be provided between the bicycle lane and the adjacent general-purpose lane (see Drawing C in Figure 9E-5).

In practice, the 100-foot stop-back for bicycle lane markings before a mandatory turn lane (Paragraph 4) is a common field-check item — verify the bicycle symbol/arrow reappears on the far side of the turn lane per Paragraph 6, not just that the near-side marking stops.

9E.03Extensions of Bicycle Lanes through Intersections (Class II Bikeways)

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Extensions of bicycle lanes through intersections can help identify the paths of bicyclists and guide them on movements that could be difficult to discern. Extensions also assist other road users of the intersection to identify where bicyclists are expected to operate and to recognize potentially unexpected conflict points.
  2. 02The design, placement, and maintenance of bicycle lane extensions through intersections are important considerations, especially when contiguous to a crosswalk, to avoid potential confusion to pedestrians with vision disabilities.
  3. 03The width and color of lane extension markings are discussed in Section 3B.11.
Option — Permissive
  1. 04The bicycle symbol, the arrow marking, pavement word markings, or a combination thereof may be used in bicycle lane extensions through intersections.
  2. 05Green-colored pavement may be used in a bicycle lane extension in accordance with the provisions of Section 3H.06.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 06Shared-lane markings or chevron markings shall not be used in bicycle lanes or bicycle lane extensions (see Section 9E.09).
  2. 07Extensions of bicycle lanes through intersections shall use dotted line patterns.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 08Separated and buffer-separated bicycle lanes may have alignments that are not as obvious within an intersection as a standard bicycle lane, therefore additional conspicuity is important where these types of bicycle lanes cross intersections.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 09Lane extension markings should be used to extend a buffer-separated or separated bicycle lane through intersections and driveways.
  2. 10The extension of a bicycle lane through an intersection should use two lines defining both lateral limits of the bicycle lane.
Option — Permissive
  1. 10aThe Bike Lane Intersection (Detail 39A) line as shown in Figure 3A-112(CA) may be used to extend the bike lane to or through an intersection.
  2. 10bThe extension of bicycle lanes through intersections advises motorists that bicyclists are likely to use the intended path.
  3. 10cBicycle lane markings may be extended through intersections consistent with the provisions of Section 3B.11.
  4. 10dBicycle lane markings as shown in Figure 9E-103(CA) may be used within the boundaries of bicycle lane extensions.
  5. 10eGreen-colored pavement may be used in a bicycle lane extension through an intersection or a driveway consistent with Section 3H.06. Refer to Figures 9E-2, 9E-2(CA), 9E-3, 9E-3(CA), 9E-8, 9E-102(CA), 9E-103(CA), and 9E-106(CA).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 11Where the path of the bicycle lane through the intersection is contiguous to a crosswalk, two longitudinal dotted lines shall be provided to establish the lateral limits of the bicycle lane extension. The transverse line establishing one side of the crosswalk, or the limit of a high-visibility crosswalk pattern (see Section 3C.05) that does not employ a transverse line, shall not be used to demarcate one side of the bicycle lane extension.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 12Markings for a bike lane through a typical interchange are shown in Figure 9E-102(CA).
Option — Permissive
  1. 13Figure 9E-102(CA) may also be used where the preferred designation is a Class III Bikeway (Bike Route), with the Bike Lane (R3-17) signs being replaced with Bike Route (D11-1) signs and the bike lane delineation eliminated. A 4- to 6-inch-wide stripe on local agency roadways, and a 6-inch-wide stripe on state highways, may be used to delineate the shoulder throughout the bike route designation.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 14Signing and striping as shown in Figure 9E-102(CA) shall be repeated at additional onramps within the interchange.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 15Where the onramps intersect at the local road at or near 90 degrees, the striping should be per Figure 9E-3 and Figure 9E-3(CA).

9E.04Bicycle Lanes at Driveways (Class II Bikeways)

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01The definition of an "Intersection" in Section 1C.02 contains information to determine if a driveway can be considered an intersection.
Option — Permissive
  1. 02Bicycle lanes may be continued through a driveway using solid or dotted longitudinal lines.
  2. 03The bicycle symbol, the arrow marking, pavement word markings, or a combination thereof may be used in bicycle lane extensions through driveways.
  3. 04Green-colored pavement (see Section 3H.06) may be used as a background to enhance the conspicuity of the bicycle symbol at driveways.

9E.05Bicycle Lanes at Circular Intersections (Class II Bikeways)

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 01Bicycle lanes shall not be provided in the circulatory roadway of an unsignalized circular intersection that includes conflicts at entry or exit points (see Chapter 3D), except as provided in Paragraph 4 of this Section.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 02Bicycle lane markings should stop at least 100 feet before the crosswalk, or if no crosswalk is provided, at least 100 feet before the yield line, or if no yield line is provided, then at least 100 feet before the edge of the circulatory roadway.
  2. 03If used, bicycle crossings should be a minimum of 20 feet from the edge of the circular roadway.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 03aRefer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for an error in the Paragraph 3 text. Refer to Section 1A.04 for more details.
Option — Permissive
  1. 04Separated bicycle lanes may be used in circular intersections.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 05Separated bicycle lanes allow bicycles to navigate a circular intersection and its crossing points without merging into traffic and without dismounting and using a crosswalk at the intersection crossing point. This is beneficial at multi-lane and higher-speed circular intersections.
  2. 06Section 9E.10 contains information on using shared-lane markings to facilitate the bicycle movement through a circular intersection.
  3. 07The "Guide for the Development of Bicycle Facilities," 2012 Fourth Edition, American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials, contains information on designing for bicycles on the sidewalk in lieu of, or in addition to, using shared-lane markings in the circulatory roadway of the intersection.
  4. 08The FHWA's informational guide "Improving Intersections for Pedestrians and Bicyclists" contains information on incorporating separated bicycle lanes and other bicycle facilities into circular intersections.
  5. 09Refer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for an error in the Paragraph 8 text. Refer to Section 1A.04 for more details.

9E.06Buffer-Separated Bicycle Lanes (Class II Bikeway)

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 00aRefer to Sections 1C.02 and SHC § 890.4.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 00bAll topics in this section referencing buffer, buffer area, buffer space, or buffer-separated bicycle lane shall be understood to apply to a Class II Bikeway, bike lane, bicycle lane, or buffer-separated bicycle lane.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Buffer-separated bicycle lanes provide additional lateral separation between a bicycle lane and a general-purpose lane by a pattern of pavement markings without the presence of vertical elements. Providing a buffer space between a bicycle lane and a general-purpose lane creates more separation between motor vehicles and bicycles, can reduce vehicle encroachment into the bicycle lane, and can increase the comfort of bicyclists.
  2. 02Providing a buffer space between a bicycle lane and a parking lane can reduce crashes involving bicycles and the opening of vehicle doors from the parking lane.
Option — Permissive
  1. 02aA bicycle lane buffer area may be used to separate a bicycle lane from an adjacent general-purpose lane and/or parking lane.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 02bBIKE LANE (R3-17) signs (refer to Figure 9B-1) should be used to distinguish a buffer-separated bicycle lane from a general-purpose lane.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 03If used, and except as provided in Paragraph 5 of this Section, a buffer space shall be marked with a solid white line along both edges of the buffer space where crossing is discouraged.
  2. 03aDirectional arrows shall be used in conjunction with the bicycle lane symbol or word marking in buffer-separated bicycle lanes, placed downstream from the symbol or word marking.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 04Engineering judgment should be used to establish intermittent breaks or interruptions in the buffer space, such as for driveways, transit stops, or on-street parallel parking lanes, in order to convey access points or an otherwise general legal movement to cross the buffer space (see Figure 9E-6 and Figure 9E-6(CA)).
Option — Permissive
  1. 05Buffer spaces may be established without specific longitudinal lines if contiguous facilities have longitudinal lines or other pavement markings themselves that, when installed, automatically demarcate the buffer space (see Drawing D in Figure 9E-6).
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 06Except as provided in Paragraph 7 of this Section, a through buffer-separated bicycle lane shall not be positioned to the right of a mandatory right-turn lane or to the left of a mandatory left-turn lane.
Option — Permissive
  1. 07A buffer-separated bicycle lane may be placed to the right of a mandatory right-turn lane (or to the left of a mandatory left-turn lane) only if a bicycle signal face (see Section 4H.01) is used and the signal phasing and signing eliminate any potential conflicts between the bicycle movement and the turning movement.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 07aWhere vehicles are expected to cross the buffer area at driveways, both lines should be broken. Otherwise, both lines should be solid.
  2. 07bBuffer areas should end on the approach to the intersection of side streets or major commercial driveways as shown in Figure 9E-6 and Figure 9E-6(CA).
  3. 08The width of the buffer space should be at least 3 times the width of the normal or wide longitudinal line used to mark the buffer space.
  4. 09Where a buffer space is 2 to 3 feet wide, chevron or diagonal markings (see Section 3B.25) should be applied within the buffer space.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 09aThe buffer area width includes the width of the parallel white lines. Refer to DIB 89-02 for buffer area width requirements.
  2. 09bMarkings for buffered bicycle lanes are shown in Figure 9E-6 and Figure 9E-6(CA).
Option — Permissive
  1. 10Where a buffer space is less than 2 feet wide, diagonal markings or no markings at all may be applied within the buffer space.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 11If used, diagonal markings shall slant away from traffic in the adjacent travel lane for motor-vehicle traffic.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 12Where used, the spacing of chevrons or diagonal markings should be 10 feet or greater.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 13Chevron and diagonal markings convey that the buffer space is not an additional bicycle lane or other travel lane open to traffic.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 14Where a buffer space is more than 3 feet wide, chevron or diagonal markings shall be applied within the buffer space.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 14aThe use of chevron or diagonal markings should be considered in a bicycle lane buffer area and should be based on Section 3B.25 and engineering judgment.
  2. 14bIf used, interior chevron or diagonal markings should consist of 4- to 6-inch lines on local agency roadways and 6-inch lines on state highways, angled at 45 degrees and striped at intervals of 10 to 40 feet.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 14cIncreased interior chevron or diagonal marking frequency can increase motorist compliance.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 15Lane extension markings should be used to extend a buffer-separated bicycle lane across intersections and driveways.

9E.07Separated Bicycle Lanes (Class IV Bikeway (Cycle Track or Separated Bikeways))

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 00aRefer to Sections 1C.02, 9E.06, and SHC § 890.4.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 00bAll topics in this section referencing buffer, buffer area, and buffer space for a buffer-separated bicycle lane shall be understood to apply to a Class II Bikeway. All topics in this section referencing buffer, buffer area, and buffer space for a separated bicycle lane shall be understood to apply to a Class IV Bikeway (Cycle Track or Separated Bikeway).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Separated bicycle lanes provide a physical separation between a general-purpose lane and a bicycle lane through the use of vertical objects or vertical separation. Providing physical separation between a bicycle lane and a general-purpose lane can reduce vehicle encroachment into the bicycle lane beyond a marked buffer alone and can in some cases prevent that encroachment altogether.
  2. 02Physical separation between general-purpose lanes and bicycle lanes introduces additional design considerations over buffer-separated bicycle lanes, including the awareness of a potentially unexpected conflict point for turning motor vehicles and the provision of adequate sight distance for all users at intersections and driveway crossings.
Option — Permissive
  1. 03Vertical elements used to provide physical separation between general-purpose lanes and bicycle lanes may include, but are not limited to, tubular markers, raised islands, parked vehicles, grade separation, flexible delineator posts, bikeway separator posts, inflexible physical barriers, or on-street parking.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 03aRefer to Figure 9E-106(CA). Refer to Caltrans' Design Information Bulletin Number 89, Class IV Bikeway Guidance (Separated Bikeways/Cycle Tracks) for more information.
  2. 03bRefer to FHWA's "Separated Bike Lane Planning and Design Guide" for detailed information on planning and design of separated bike lanes.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 03cVertical elements, vertical objects, or vertical separation between the general-purpose lane and bicycle lane shall be used to define a Class IV Bikeway (cycle track, separated bikeway, or separated bicycle lane).
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 03dVertical elements are not traffic control devices in themselves; however, when placed in a position identical to a line of channelizing devices and marked and/or equipped with appropriate channelization features to provide guidance and warning both day and night, they function as traffic control devices.
  2. 04Where on-street parking is provided adjacent to the buffer area of a separated bicycle lane, pedestrians will need to access those vehicles.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 05BIKE LANE (R3-17) signs (see Figure 9B-1) should be used to distinguish a separated bicycle lane from a general-purpose lane. Refer to Paragraph 00a and 00b in this section.

9E.08Counter-Flow Bicycle Lanes (Class II Bikeway or Class IV Bikeway)

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Counter-flow bicycle lanes are one-directional and provide a lawful path of travel for bicycles in the opposite direction from general traffic on a roadway that allows general traffic to travel in only one direction.
  2. 02Counter-flow bicycle lanes establish two-way traffic on a roadway. Section 9B.21 contains information on the Left Turn Yield to Bicycles (R10-12b) sign used with traffic signals and counter-flow bicycle lanes.
  3. 02aCounter-flow bicycle lanes can be provided as a Class II Bikeway (bike lane, bicycle lane, or buffer-separated bicycle lane) or as a Class IV Bikeway (cycle track, separated bikeway, or separated bicycle lane), depending upon the physical separation provided between a general-purpose lane and a bicycle lane. If the separation is provided by a pattern of pavement markings without vertical elements, it is a Class II Bikeway; if the separation is provided by vertical objects or vertical separation, it is a Class IV Bikeway. Refer to Sections 1C.02, 9E.06, 9E.07, and SHC § 890.4.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 03Where used, a counter-flow bicycle lane should be marked such that bicycles in the counter-flow lane travel on their right-hand side of the road in accordance with normal rules of the road, with opposing traffic on the left.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 04Counter-flow bicycle lanes located at the edge of the roadway shall use double yellow center line pavement markings (see Section 3B.01), a painted median island, a raised median island (see Chapter 3J), or some form of physical separation where the speed limit is 30 mph or less.
  2. 05For speed limits 35 mph or greater, a buffer per Section 3B.25, a painted or raised median island, or some form of physical separation shall be used to separate a counter-flow bicycle lane from the adjacent travel lane.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 06Lane extension markings should be used where counter-flow bicycle movements cross intersections.
  2. 07Counter-flow bicycle lanes should not be used between a general-purpose lane and an on-street parallel parking lane for motor vehicles.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 08Counter-flow bicycle lanes located between a general-purpose lane and an on-street parallel parking lane for motor vehicles can limit visibility of bicycles for vehicles exiting the parking lane, potentially impacting the safety of bicyclists. Locating counter-flow bicycle lanes at the edge of the roadway can reduce conflicts for bicycles.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 09Where signs are provided to regulate turns from streets or driveways that intersect with a roadway that has a counter-flow bicycle lane, ONE WAY signs (see Section 2B.49) shall not be used. Movement Prohibition signs (see Section 2B.26) with supplemental EXCEPT BICYCLES (R3-7bP) regulatory plaque(s) shall be used (see Figure 9E-8).
  2. 10If a DO NOT ENTER (R5-1) sign package is used at egress points for motor vehicle traffic, the EXCEPT BICYCLES (R3-7bP) regulatory plaque(s) shall be placed under the DO NOT ENTER sign (see Figure 9E-8) where a counter-flow bicycle lane is used.
  3. 11Where intersection traffic controls are provided (such as STOP or YIELD signs or traffic signals), appropriate devices shall be provided and oriented toward bicyclists in the counter-flow lane.
  4. 12At signalized locations, appropriate bicycle signalization (see Chapter 9F) shall be provided and oriented toward bicyclists in the counter-flow lane, including a method for counter-flow bicycles to actuate the green phase for the counter-flow movement.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 13Higher levels of traffic control or additional signalization, signing, and/or pavement marking treatments can be helpful for intersecting traffic where the counter-flow bicycle movement is unexpected.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 14A Bicycle Cross Traffic warning plaque (see Section 9C.06) should be used below a STOP sign on the crossroad at intersections where a counter-flow bicycle lane is provided on the primary street.

In practice, the 30 mph / 35 mph threshold in Paragraphs 4–5 mirrors the threshold used elsewhere in this chapter for dashed-stripe length — treat 30 mph and below as the "light separation" tier (double yellow or median island) and 35 mph and above as the "buffer or physical separation required" tier.

9E.12Bicycle Box

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Option — Permissive
  1. 01A bicycle box (see Figure 9E-12) may be used to increase the visibility of stopped bicycles on the approach to a signalized intersection during the portion of the signal cycle when a red signal indication is being displayed to motor vehicles in the approach lane(s) behind the box.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 02Providing a bicycle box on a signalized intersection approach where a discernible number of conflicts between vehicles turning across through bicycles in a bicycle lane has been demonstrated during the green interval of a signal should be evaluated based on engineering judgment or study.

Option — Permissive
  1. 12Green-colored pavement may be used in a bicycle box.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 13If used, green-colored pavement shall be used in the full limits of the bicycle box.

Barrier Posts on Class I Bikeways (Bike Path)

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 08Before a decision is made to install barrier posts, consideration needs to be given to the implementation of other remedial measures, such as Bike Path Exclusion (R44A(CA)) signs (refer to Section 9B.07) and/or redesigning the path entry so that motorists do not confuse it with vehicle access.

Barrier post installations should be designed so they are removable to permit entrance by emergency and service vehicles.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 11Generally, barrier configurations that preclude entry by motorcycles present safety and convenience problems for bicyclists. Such devices are used only where extreme problems are encountered.

9E.17Raised Devices (Class IV Bikeway (Cycle Track, Separated Bikeway or Separated Bicycle Lane))

Standard · Guidance · Option · Support — paraphrased faithfully below, organized by paragraph type.

Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 01Chapter 3I contains information on using channelizing devices to emphasize pavement marking patterns associated with certain bicycle facilities. A common application is the use of flexible raised devices to create separated bicycle lanes (see Section 9E.07).
  2. 02Using inflexible raised devices immediately adjacent to the travel path of a bicyclist without a buffer creates a collision potential for bicyclists.
Option — Permissive
  1. 03In accordance with Chapter 3I, channelizing devices may be used to emphasize a pavement marking pattern that establishes a bicycle lane or other bicycle facility, provided that the installation of channelizing devices does not prevent motor vehicles from turning when the turn requires the motor vehicle to merge with the bicycle lane or facility as required by law or ordinance.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 04If used, channelizing devices for bicycle facilities should be tubular markers (see Section 3I.02).
  2. 05The selection of a raised device for use with bicycle facilities should consider the collision potential of both the post and the base, since the base might still be present in the event the post is struck and missing.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 06Measures to reduce the likelihood of a road user striking a channelizing device include marking a buffer space, improving lighting, improving retroreflectivity, or the periodic addition of taller vertical elements within runs of shorter elements.
Standard — Mandatory Requirement
  1. 07Channelizing devices that are used to emphasize the pavement marking patterns of bicycle facilities shall not incorporate the color green into either the device or its retroreflective element to supplement the presence of green-colored pavement.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 07aRefer to Sections 3A.03 and 3I.01 for the color of channelizing devices, when used in bicycle facilities.
Guidance — Recommended Practice
  1. 08If used in buffer-separated bicycle lanes, channelizing devices should be placed in the buffer space and at least 1 foot from the longitudinal bicycle lane pavement marking.
Support — Background/Explanatory
  1. 09Refer to FHWA's List of Known Errors for an error in the Paragraph 08 text. Refer to Section 1A.04 for more details.

★ Key Takeaways — Chapter 9E

  • Chapter 9E covers pavement markings across all four bikeway classes: Class I (shared-use path, 9E.13), Class II (bike lane / buffer-separated bicycle lane, 9E.01–9E.06, 9E.08), Class III (bike route, 9E.14), and Class IV (separated bikeway/cycle track, 9E.07–9E.08, 9E.17).
  • The Class II vs. Class IV distinction turns entirely on whether separation is provided by pavement-marking patterns alone (buffer-separated, Section 9E.06) or by vertical elements/objects (separated, Section 9E.07) — this determines which set of buffer-width and turn-lane rules applies.
  • A through bicycle lane may never be positioned to the right of a right-turn-only lane or left of a left-turn-only lane except where a bicycle signal (Chapter 4H) controls the movement (Section 9E.02).
  • Key dimensional thresholds to remember: dashed bike-lane stripe length 50–200 ft depending on block length/speed (9E.01); 100-ft bicycle-lane stop-back before a mandatory turn lane and before circular-intersection crosswalks (9E.02, 9E.05); buffer width triggers for chevron/diagonal markings at 2 ft and 3 ft (9E.06, 9E.07); shared-lane marking speed ceiling of 30 mph standard / 40 mph guidance and spacing of 50–250 ft (9E.09); 10-ft minimum bicycle box depth (9E.12).
  • Mixing zones (9E.02) are permitted only for one-way bicycle lanes running with adjacent traffic, require yield markings, and must carry shared-lane markings plus turn arrows where they continue to the intersection.
  • Two-stage bicycle turn boxes (9E.11) and bicycle boxes (9E.12) are both Option-level treatments with strict Standard-level geometry and signal-timing requirements once installed — turns on red must be prohibited across both.
  • Raised barriers and raised pavement markers are prohibited for delineating Class II bike lanes (9E.01) but vertical elements are required to create a Class IV separated bikeway (9E.07, 9E.17); channelizing devices used for bicycle facilities may never incorporate green color (9E.17).
  • Green-colored pavement is optional throughout most bicycle facility markings but is explicitly prohibited as a background for shared-lane markings (9E.09) and, where used in a two-stage turn box or bicycle box, must cover the box's full limits (9E.11, 9E.12).
